Case study · AI Engineer · Wholesale e-commerce

An LLM sales assistant, shipped to a wholesale floor

Two months at Atacado Connect as sole AI engineer: an internal assistant that answers a seller in plain language — “customer wants something for gaming between 200 and 300” — with real products only, each carrying its SKU, price and stock. A fine-tuned Qwen3-8B self-hosted on one RTX 5090, lexical retrieval over a ~20K-SKU catalog, and the evaluation harness that gated every release — including the two versions it refused.

Overview

  • Shipped an internal LLM sales agent to production for a wholesale sales team — natural-language product search over a 22.4K-product catalog, answering with real SKUs, prices and stock, and never inventing a product or a delivery date.
  • Fine-tuned Qwen3-8B with QLoRA (LoRA r=32; 87M trainable parameters, 1.05% of the model) on 2,172 curated multi-turn tool-calling trajectories, lifting a 60-case held-out benchmark from 88.3% to 95.0% with zero critical failures — and cutting the model’s dependence on scaffolding, the gap between the guarded agent and the bare model, from 21.6 to 6.7 points.
  • Built the release gate — 60 held-out cases across 17 dimensions, fully mechanical graders, critical-failure weighting and automatic abort on train/test contamination — and ran it on five fine-tuned versions. It blocked two of my own models from production, with the causal write-up to justify each call.
  • Designed a self-verifying retrieval benchmark: 26,594 generated queries over 8,605 products, where the product itself is the ground truth — zero human annotation, about US$4 to build.
  • Self-hosted the model on vLLM (FP8, prefix caching, 16K context) on a single RTX 5090: p50 2.2s end-to-end at 128.7 tok/s across 309 real production turns, and 80 concurrent agent loops at 2.71 q/s with zero errors in load testing.
  • Sole author across the whole stack — data cleaning, retrieval engine, fine-tuning pipeline, serving, backend and frontend: 278 commits and ~60K hand-written lines in seven weeks, with 23 offline test scripts and ~600 assertions.

Two products

In production

Internal assistant for the sales floor

A chat assistant the sellers use while a customer is on the line. It reads the request in Spanish or Portuguese, searches the live catalog, and answers with real products only — SKU, price and stock on every line.

  • Tool-use agent loop, provider-agnostic — the same code runs against the local model or a frontier API, which is what makes comparing them honest.
  • 16 named response guardrails re-check the finished answer against what the tools actually returned: invented SKUs, prices copied off the wrong product, out-of-stock recommendations.
  • Lexical retrieval (SQLite FTS5/BM25) with deterministic attribute filters for RAM, storage, screen size and battery.
  • Streaming responses, admin panel, review queue, and a step-by-step X-ray of the internal pipeline for debugging.

Deployed to a ~70-person sales team · 56 used it · peak day 28

Built · not deployed

AI product finder for the storefront

A customer-facing version of the same brain: you type “I want a notebook” on the public site and it returns real product cards instead of a keyword result page. Built on the same fine-tuned model and retrieval stack as the internal assistant.

  • Natural-language search that returns product cards, not a list of links.
  • Side-by-side comparison of two products and their specs.
  • Add to cart and save to favourites, straight from the answer.
  • Shareable links for a single product or a whole cart.
  • Voice input — a Whisper large-v3-turbo sharing the same GPU as the language model.

What I actually learned

Most of the results I am proudest of are negative, and measured. Each of these is a decision I made not to ship something, backed by a number.

I blocked two of my own models
Versions 4 and 4.1 scored better as standalone models and worse inside the production agent, with a repeatable Portuguese jailbreak regression. That pattern told me the SFT-mixture lever was exhausted rather than mis-tuned — so I wrote up why instead of shipping a higher number.
I killed a feature before building it
I computed a neural re-ranker’s ceiling first: +0.3 points. 96% of failures had no correct product anywhere in the top-50 — a recall problem, not a ranking one. The re-ranker was never built.
I ran GRPO, and it failed — measurably
120 steps, 7.6M tokens: 85.8% of steps had zero reward variance, so there was no policy gradient at all, with 53.7% of completions truncated and a non-zero reference KL exposing the wrong reference policy. Upstream, a 665-prompt screen showed 66.9% were already solved 8 times out of 8 — supervised training had saturated the signal RL needed. I halted the pipeline at its data gate instead of spending GPU.
Embeddings were a measured “no”
I ran multilingual-e5-large (1024-dim) on ONNX, no PyTorch, and recommended against adopting it. The vector space clusters by brand at 95% (18% by chance — 5.3×) but by RAM at only 59% (34% by chance — 1.7×): it organises by manufacturer, not by specification. On exact numeric specs it reached 50% precision@10 against 100% from the deterministic filter I already had. I also showed no vector database was warranted — searching 22,427 vectors takes 2.2 ms in numpy. No FAISS, no Chroma.
The real bottleneck was adoption, not the model
Instrumenting the guardrails told me something I did not want to hear: 6 human votes across 327 responses. With that little signal I could not tell whether any change helped, so I stopped shipping model changes and said so. Knowing a metric is too thin to act on is worth more than another point of benchmark.
